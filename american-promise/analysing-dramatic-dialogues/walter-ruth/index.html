What is said?

Understanding the conflict

Before examining techniques, establish what is at stake.

A dramatic dialogue is not simply a collection of individual statements. Its meaning often emerges from how one character responds to another. As you read, look for the difference between a character's immediate concerns and what they ultimately hope to achieve.

Act I, Scene I · pp. 8, l. 15 – 10, l. 35

Start with the scene

  1. What is Walter trying to achieve, and why is this so important to him?
  2. What is Ruth primarily concerned about? What does she expect from Walter in this conversation?
  3. Where do their expectations clash? Distinguish the immediate disagreement from the deeper underlying conflict.
Need a starting point?
Ask yourself: What does Walter want Ruth to engage with? What practical concerns keep drawing Ruth's attention? Consider what these priorities reveal about their different situations, rather than simply labelling either character as right or wrong.
From observation to interpretation: The important question is not only what they disagree about, but why they seem unable to understand each other's position.
02 / 04
How is it said?

Examining interaction & language

Two guided close readings — then make your own observations.

The way a conversation unfolds can tell the audience as much as the words themselves. Pay attention to who speaks, how the other person responds and how language and stage directions work together.

Close Reading A · Who gets heard?

p. 8, ll. 18–35

Turn-taking describes how speaking turns are distributed and how the speakers respond to each other. Notice who speaks at length, who answers briefly and whether the characters engage with one another's concerns.

Name it → Prove it → Explain it
  1. Name it: Compare Walter's and Ruth's contributions. What differences do you notice in their length, content and responses?
  2. Prove it: Identify one revealing sequence of speaking turns and provide precise line references.
  3. Explain it: What does this pattern reveal about their priorities and their relationship? How does it contribute to the growing tension?
How do I analyse turn-taking?
Avoid writing only “There is turn-taking.” Explain a pattern and its function. For example: While X speaks at length about …, Y responds by … . This contrasting pattern of turn-taking highlights …

Close Reading B · When ordinary words gain weight

p. 9, ll. 33–38 & p. 10, ll. 1–16

A repeated everyday phrase can acquire additional meaning as a conversation becomes more tense. Its subtext may be shaped by the situation, reactions and changes in tone.

  1. Name it: What is striking about the recurring reference to the eggs?
  2. Prove it: Compare at least two moments when eggs are mentioned. Include relevant stage directions.
  3. Explain it: How does the significance of this seemingly ordinary topic change during the exchange? What does it suggest about their relationship?
Look beyond the literal meaning
Ask what each character is really reacting to. Do not assume that “eggs” is an English idiom for courage or masculinity. The additional meaning emerges from the dialogue, not from a fixed expression.
Your turn: Find two further revealing moments in the passage. Consider a response, word choice, gesture, shift in tone or stage direction. Explain what each reveals; do not simply list techniques.

How does it develop?

Tracing dramatic progression

Follow the exchange as an evolving conflict.

A conversation can become more confrontational through a series of responses, rather than through a single dramatic statement. Escalation means that the tension or intensity of the conflict increases. A turning point is a moment when the direction or dynamics of the exchange change significantly.

Map the conversation

  1. How does the discussion begin? What is the immediate topic?
  2. Where does the interaction become more tense? Identify a specific response, line or stage direction that marks a change.
  3. How does Walter react as he feels increasingly unheard? How does Ruth respond?
  4. How does the conversation end? Does either character seem to have achieved what they wanted?
Make connections: Return to your observations about turn-taking, repetition, tone and stage directions. Explain how these features work together to develop the conflict.
What if there is no single turning point?
That is fine. Not every dialogue contains a clearly identifiable turning point. You may trace a gradual change in tension instead. Support whichever interpretation fits the text best.
