UNDERSTAND

Checkpoint 01 / 04

What is said?

Understanding the conflict before analysing its presentation

Before examining individual techniques, find out what is at stake. A conflict becomes meaningful when we understand what the characters want from each other — and why they may not be able to provide it.

Your reading

Read the complete exchange between Walter and Ruth. Focus on their concerns and expectations rather than collecting character traits.

p. 8, ll. 15–35p. 9, ll. 1–38p. 10, ll. 1–35
1

Two different concerns: What does Walter want Ruth to understand or do? What appears to matter most to Ruth in this situation? Make short notes for both perspectives.

2

Behind the disagreement: What causes their frustration? Distinguish the immediate topic of the argument from any deeper expectations or pressures.

3

First evidence: Mark one line for each character that helps explain their position. Record the page and line numbers.

Need a small nudge?

Think about Walter's plans, Ruth's daily responsibilities and the way both respond to not feeling understood. Avoid deciding too early which character is 'right'.

Keep this question in mind

Is the argument simply about Walter's business idea — or also about what he and Ruth expect from their marriage?

ANALYSE

Checkpoint 02 / 04

How is it said?

Looking closely at interaction, language and stage directions

A dramatic dialogue works through responses. To analyse it, look at how a statement is answered, ignored, challenged or repeated — and what happens as a result.

Close Reading A · Who gets heard?

Focus on turn-taking

Turn-taking is the pattern of speaking turns: who speaks at length, who responds briefly, whether speakers engage with each other and how control of the exchange shifts.

p. 8, ll. 18–35p. 9, ll. 5–21

Look closely at a sequence of several exchanges. Then move from observation to interpretation:

NAME ITDescribe a noticeable pattern in the speaking turns.
PROVE ITCompare two connected contributions and give line references.
EXPLAIN ITWhat might this pattern reveal about the characters' relationship?
1

Compare the length and function of Walter's and Ruth's contributions. Who develops an idea? Who responds briefly? Do the replies address what was said?

2

Find two linked speaking turns that illustrate your observation. Explain how Ruth's response affects Walter's next contribution (or vice versa).

Analysis support: How can I phrase this?

Walter's extended remarks contrast with Ruth's ...

Instead of addressing ..., Ruth ... . This may suggest ...

The pattern of turn-taking reveals ... because ...

Be precise: speaking more does not automatically mean having more power. Interpret the entire exchange.

Close Reading B · When ordinary words gain weight

Follow one recurring expression

Track Ruth's reference to the eggs and Walter's responses. Notice how repetition, tone and stage directions change the force of an everyday remark.

p. 9, ll. 33–38p. 10, ll. 1–17
3

Name it: What changes in the way the eggs are mentioned? Notice who repeats the words and how the characters' tone or behaviour changes.

4

Prove it: Choose two moments and include relevant stage directions. How is each moment different?

5

Explain it: Why does the reference to the eggs become so charged? What does it suggest about what the characters are really arguing about?

Think beyond a simple interpretation

Walter interprets Ruth's practical response in one way. But what do her own words and actions suggest about her perspective? Your explanation should take both characters seriously.

Useful analytical language: The repetition of ... gains significance as ... / The stage direction indicating ... suggests ... / By contrasting ... with ..., Hansberry reveals ...
TRACE

Checkpoint 03 / 04

How does it develop?

Tracing the dynamics of the conversation — and exploring independently

Dramatic conflicts are rarely static. A conversation may begin with everyday remarks, shift into personal accusations or reach a moment when the tension becomes impossible to ignore. Trace the progression rather than listing isolated devices.

Map the conversation

Earlier in the exchange

Walter tries to introduce his plans, while Ruth responds to him in different ways. p. 8, ll. 18–35

Later in the exchange

Walter's emotional reaction intensifies, and both characters express deeper grievances. p. 10, ll. 1–35

1

Describe two or three phases of the conversation in your own words. At what point does the exchange noticeably change direction or intensity?

2

Explore on your own: Find two additional revealing details that you have not yet analysed closely. Choose at least one example involving interaction or stage directions and one involving language or tone.

3

Use Name it → Prove it → Explain it for both details. How do they contribute to the progression of the conflict?

4

Does the exchange contain a meaningful turning point — a moment after which the interaction takes a different direction? If so, justify your choice. If not, explain how the tension develops more gradually.

Need possible places to revisit?

You could examine Ruth's longer response near the end, Walter's statements about men and women, or the final stage directions describing Ruth's actions. Choose only what supports your interpretation.

Think critically

Has either character's position changed by the end — or have their conflicting expectations become clearer? Both are possible findings; support your answer with the text.

Step back & teach

Bring your analysis together — and make your group's insight useful to others

The other groups have examined different family conflicts. Your job is to explain what makes Walter and Ruth's conflict distinctive and demonstrate an analytical approach the others can use in their own scenes.

3
