Skills training: Analysing (political) speeches

Political speeches are usually carefully crafted: speakers choose their words to influence a particular audience in a particular situation. A speech may aim to raise awareness, promote a vision, convince listeners or challenge them to act. Often, several of these aims overlap.

Before analysing the speech, establish its communicative situation.

Who?
Who is speaking, and what is their role at this point in time?
When and on what occasion?
Was the speech given before an election, after a major event or during a public debate?
Where?
In which country and at what kind of venue was the speech delivered?
To whom?
Who is the audience: supporters, opponents, politicians, workers, students or the wider public?
With which aim?
Does the speaker want to thank, convince, inspire, reassure, warn or challenge the audience?
On what topic?
What issue, vision, problem or plan does the speaker address?
Example: introductory sentence

The excerpt from Senator Barack Obama’s political speech “Change We Can Believe In”, his Democratic nomination victory speech delivered on 3 June 2008 in St Paul, Minnesota, aims to convince the audience that he is the right candidate for the 2008 presidential election and outlines his goals and beliefs.

Useful elements for your introductory sentence

  • speaker and role
  • title and text type
  • occasion, place and date
  • audience and topic
  • central communicative aim

Reconstruct how the speaker develops the central idea from beginning to end.

1

Introduction: The speaker attracts attention and establishes the purpose and topic, for example by asking a question, telling a short story, pointing out a problem or referring to a current event, object, image or statistic.

2

Main part: The speaker develops the main ideas step by step, supports them with evidence and background information, illustrates them with examples or personal experience and may propose solutions.

3

Conclusion: The speaker often summarises key ideas, presents an outlook, reaffirms a vision or ends with an emotional appeal or call for action.

Common movement: Many speeches begin with a specific example, event or story and then move towards a more general problem, idea or vision.

Useful language for outlining the argument

  • The speaker starts off with / begins by ...
  • The speaker provides several arguments / facts for ...
  • They compare ... with ... / illustrate ... / criticise ...
  • They claim / state / concede / admit that ...
  • They challenge / question / refute the idea that ...
  • They focus on / provide background information about ...
  • They suggest / offer a solution to ...
  • The argument shifts from ... to ...
  • The speaker concludes by suggesting / reaffirming / appealing for ...
Speakers use different communicative strategies to convey their message effectively, influence how it is received and engage their audience.

What these strategies may achieve

Build a connection

through inclusive pronouns, personal stories, conversational language or humour

Make ideas accessible

through examples, details, stories and careful simplification

Increase credibility

through facts, statistics, expert opinions and quotations

Place emphasis

through striking details, examples, illustrations or repetition

Maintain attention

through surprising stories, questions, direct address or jokes

Shape the speaker’s image

as competent, relatable, witty, trustworthy, strong or compassionate

Common communicative strategies

When analysing a speech, first identify which communicative strategy the speaker uses. Then explain its effect on the audience and how it contributes to the speaker’s overall aim. Common strategies include:

Pronouns

Inclusive we/us can create unity; we/us versus they/them may create opposing groups. Direct address with you involves the listener. Frequent I may present the speaker as active, responsible or powerful.

Concrete examples and details

Specific, authentic or current cases can illustrate an issue, emphasise its relevance and make an abstract problem easier to understand.

Personal anecdotes

Stories can make an issue relatable, create an emotional connection and present the speaker as approachable or “one of us”.

Authority and evidence

Expert opinions, quotations, facts and statistics can strengthen credibility and make an argument appear well founded.

Background information

Explanations can demonstrate competence and help the audience understand a complex topic.

Questions

Direct or rhetorical questions can attract attention, invite reflection and challenge listeners to consider their own position.

Generalisation and simplification

Sweeping statements can reduce complexity and offer clear solutions, but may omit important details or alternative viewpoints.

Humour

Jokes can entertain, lighten the mood, maintain attention and strengthen the connection with the audience.

Motifs, historical references, symbols and role models

  • American speakers may draw on key ideas such as the American Dream, independence, freedom, equality, opportunity or American exceptionalism.
  • Historical references can suggest continuity, compare past and present or place the speaker within a valued tradition.
  • Symbols and places can represent larger ideas, for example the White House for the US government or Wall Street for business and finance.
  • National role models such as Martin Luther King Jr may be quoted or praised to connect the speaker’s message with courage, justice or another shared value.
Ask why this reference appears here: Does it legitimise the speaker, create shared identity, recall a national promise or connect a current proposal to an admired tradition?

Syntax and sentence structure

  • Short sentences can sound punchy, intense, urgent and memorable.
  • Long or complex sentences may sound reflective, explanatory, abstract or distancing.
  • Parallel syntax connects ideas, creates rhythm and can emphasise contrasts or key points.

Tone

The tone is the attitude or mood created by the speaker’s language. Look for evidence and notice shifts in tone.

serious · distant · impersonal

formal, factual or detached

hopeful · enthusiastic · patriotic

positive, visionary or uplifting

humorous · ironic · witty

playful, self-mocking or entertaining

authentic · vulnerable · emotional

personal, heartfelt or compassionate

concerned · worried · pessimistic

focused on dangers, problems or the future

mocking · provocative · sarcastic

ridiculing or attacking another person or view

challenging · angry · aggressive

urgent, confrontational or strongly judgemental

Do not merely label the tone: Explain which words, images or structures create it and how it supports the speaker’s purpose.

Word fields and choice of words

  • Look for semantic fields such as war, nature, religion, journey or family.
  • Examine strongly positive or negative connotations and the associations they create.
  • Consider whether the speaker uses formal, technical, colloquial, simple or emotionally charged language.

Important stylistic devices

AImages and figurative devices

Simile

A direct comparison using as or like.Example: “The nation is like a ship in a storm.”

Personification

A human quality or action is attributed to something non-human.Example: “Huge cities never sleep.”

Metaphor

An implied comparison without as or like. Political speeches often draw on familiar metaphorical fields:

  • journey: “When you’ve taken the wrong road, you don’t just keep going, you change direction.”
  • illness: “Yesterday, Wall Street suffered its worst losses since just after 9/11.”
  • game: “We don’t have victories anymore. ... I beat China all the time.”
  • battle: “We are in a fight for our principles.”
  • nature: “An economic hurricane has swept the world.”
  • machine / system: “The system is broken. I alone can fix it.”
Metonymy

A related term stands for a larger institution or idea.Example: “The White House declared ...”

BStructural devices

Alliteration

The repetition of an initial consonant sound in neighbouring words.Example: “safe and secure”

Anaphora

The repetition of the same word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or sentences.Example: “I believe in better schools. I believe in better hospitals. I believe in ...”

Antithesis / contrast

Opposing ideas are placed next to each other, often in a parallel structure.Example: “He gave everything to those who deserved it, but ... nothing to those who were idle.”

Repetition

Words or phrases are repeated to reinforce an idea and make it memorable.Example: “All are equal. All are free. All deserve a better chance.”

Parallelism

Similar grammatical structures create rhythm, connect ideas or highlight a contrast.Example: “We charge him ..., we accuse him ..., and we censure him ...”

Rule of three

Three parallel words, phrases or clauses create balance and emphasis.Example: “of the people, by the people, for the people”

Climax

Ideas are arranged in order of increasing importance or intensity.Example: “There are three things that will endure: faith, hope and love. But the greatest of these is love.”

CDevices of style and tone

Hyperbole

Deliberate exaggeration can dramatise, ridicule or entertain.Example: “No one loves women more than I do.”

Understatement

Deliberate downplaying makes something appear less important or serious than it is.Example: “The situation is not ideal.”

Pun

A play on words uses a double meaning to attract attention or create humour.Example: “Atheism is a non-prophet institution.”

Effects depend on context: A device may attract attention, create a vivid image, dramatise or simplify an issue, entertain the audience or reinforce a contrast. Do not assign an automatic effect—explain what this particular device does in this particular passage.

Build analytical paragraphs instead of listing devices.

evidence→technique→effect on the audience→contribution to the speaker’s aim

Useful analytical phrases

  • The speaker uses / employs / makes use of ...
  • The speaker alludes to / suggests that ...
  • They draw a comparison / an analogy between ...
  • They compare / contrast ... with ...
  • The speaker uses images / examples / metaphors / personification to convey a vivid impression of ...
  • The function / aim / effect of ... is to ...
  • The intention behind this ... is to ...
  • By using this device, the speaker tries to ...
  • This device serves to / helps to ...
  • The speaker illustrates the argument by ...
  • The repetition / alliteration / analogy underlines / stresses / underscores that ...
  • The speaker’s criticism / irony is directed against ...

Final checklist

  • Read the task carefully and focus on the aspect named by the operator.
  • Select relevant and powerful evidence instead of collecting random devices.
  • Group your findings logically by analytical aspect rather than working through the speech line by line.
  • Use one paragraph for each analytical aspect and connect it to the speaker’s purpose or effect on the audience.
  • Use the simple present when referring to the speech: Obama argues; Trump claims.
  • Quote briefly and accurately. Integrate quotations into your own sentences and include line references.
  • End with a conclusion that summarises the most important findings and their combined effect.
Keep it clear and logical: The quality of an analysis depends on how convincingly you explain the connection between evidence, technique, effect and overall argument—not on the number of devices you name.
