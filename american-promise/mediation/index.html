Skills training: Mediation

Mediation means conveying relevant information from a German source in English for a new communicative situation. It is not a word-for-word translation. Your task determines what to select, how to organise it and how to address your audience.

SelectChoose only the information that answers the task and is relevant for the addressee.
RestructureOrganise the information logically for the new situation. You do not have to follow the source text paragraph by paragraph.
SummariseCondense details while keeping important facts, distinctions and qualifications.
ParaphraseUse natural English and your own words. Avoid translating German structures literally.
Report accuratelyRepresent the source fairly. Do not add facts or opinions that are not supported by the source.
Bridge cultural gapsBriefly explain institutions, customs or concepts your English-speaking audience may not know.
Keep in mind: A good mediation text is shaped by purpose, addressee, context, text type and register — not by the structure of the German source.

A speech contribution is written to be heard. It should therefore sound direct, clear and audience-oriented — while still reporting the source accurately.

OpeningAddress the audience, connect to the occasion or discussion and introduce the focus of your contribution.
Main partPresent the relevant information in a clear order. Group related ideas and use transitions so listeners can follow you.
Audience connectionUse occasional direct address, signposting or a rhetorical question where it helps — but do not turn the task into a persuasive political speech.
EndingRound off your contribution with a brief conclusion, key takeaway or return to the discussion question.
Spoken style does not mean informal language.
Prefer clear sentence structure, useful signposting and direct audience reference. Avoid slang, overly complicated sentences and a long essay-style introduction.

You normally do not need a formal bibliographical reference, line references or a TATTT-style introduction. Mention the source naturally if it helps your audience understand where the information comes from.

Introducing the source
As we discuss social mobility today, I would like to draw on an article by Ciani-Sophia Hoeder published in DIE ZEIT.
Reporting a view
According to Hoeder, ...
Highlighting a point
The author points out that ...
Making attribution clear
Hoeder argues / emphasises / suggests that ...
Source or own voice? Make clear when you are reporting the author’s view. Do not present the author’s claims as your own unless the task asks for your personal evaluation.
Opening Good morning everyone. · As part of today’s discussion on ..., I would like to focus on ... · One perspective worth considering comes from ...
Structuring First of all, ... · Another important aspect is ... · What is particularly striking is ... · This also shows that ...
Explaining unfamiliar context For those who may not be familiar with ..., ... · In Germany, ... refers to ... · This is comparable to ...
Ending Taken together, these points show that ... · The key point to take away is ... · This is why the issue remains important for our discussion today.
  • Have I answered the exact focus of the task?
  • Have I selected rather than translated?
  • Is my structure clear for a listener?
  • Have I made clear whose views or findings I am reporting?
  • Have I explained cultural references only where necessary?
  • Does the text sound like a speech contribution rather than an essay?
  • Is my language accurate, neutral and appropriate for the audience?
