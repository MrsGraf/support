Skills training: Argumentative writing

The structure of your argumentative text depends on the operator. Do not use the same pattern automatically for every task.
comment (on) Defend a clear position.
discuss Weigh up both sides.
assess / evaluate Judge according to relevant criteria.
  • Read the task carefully and identify the exact issue you are expected to address.
  • Decide on your position or, for a discussion, identify the main arguments on both sides.
  • Collect a few strong arguments instead of many superficial ones.
  • Arrange your ideas in a logical order before you start writing.
Keep in mind: Your line of argument should always serve the exact focus of the task.
1

Open the topic

Start with a brief general opening statement that leads into the issue. Choose one suitable approach — you do not need to use all of them.

relevant context definition of a key term short quotation thought-provoking question
2

Narrow down the issue

Rephrase the task or central question in your own words and make clear what exactly you are commenting on, discussing or evaluating.

3

Lead into the central question

End your introduction by clearly identifying the issue you are going to address. You do not need to state your final position yet — it should become clear through your line of argument and be made explicit in the conclusion.

Do not write: “In the following, I am going to comment on …”
Example
Task: Comment on Trump’s vision of America’s future as presented in the excerpt.

Political leaders often use inaugural addresses to present a vision of what their country should become. In his 2025 inaugural address, Donald Trump describes the beginning of his presidency as a moment of national renewal and promises a new era of American strength and success. This raises the question of how convincing this vision of America’s future is.

Why this works: The introduction provides brief context, refers directly to the material named in the task and turns the task focus into a central question without already giving away the final judgement.

Develop one clear main idea per paragraph. Each paragraph should contribute directly to your overall line of argument.

claim→explanation→example / evidence→link back to the question
  • Start each paragraph with a clear topic sentence.
  • Explain why the point matters instead of merely stating it.
  • Support your argument with relevant examples, facts, knowledge or experience where appropriate.
  • Use linking words to show how your ideas relate to one another.
  • Do not add irrelevant details simply because they appear in the source text.
Comment: Counterarguments can be included and refuted if they strengthen your own position.
  • Bring your argument to a clear, well-founded conclusion.
  • Restate your central position or judgement in different words.
  • Do not introduce new arguments or evidence.
  • Where appropriate, end with a consequence, recommendation or brief outlook.

Use linking phrases selectively. They should make the logic of your argument visible, not replace it.

Adding a point

  • Moreover, …
  • Furthermore, …
  • In addition, …
  • Another important point is that …

Giving an example / evidence

  • For example, …
  • For instance, …
  • This becomes clear when …
  • A good example of this is …

Contrasting ideas

  • However, …
  • By contrast, …
  • While / Although …, …
  • On the one hand, … / On the other hand, …

Important: If you use on the one hand, you must also use on the other hand to complete the contrast.

Explaining / developing a point

  • This shows that …
  • This is important because …
  • As a result, …
  • Therefore, …

Expressing a position

  • In my view, …
  • I would argue that …
  • From my perspective, …
  • It seems convincing to argue that …

Concluding

  • Ultimately, …
  • In conclusion, …
  • All in all, …
  • Taking these arguments into account, …
