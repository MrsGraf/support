Skills training: Comprehension tasks

Comprehension tasks test your selective understanding of a text. You are not expected to reproduce everything you have understood.
read the operator→identify the focus→select relevant information→leave the rest out
  • Underline the exact focus of the task before you start writing.
  • Select only information that directly helps you answer that focus.
  • Combine related information even if it appears in different parts of the text.
  • Be concise, but make sure that all relevant aspects are included.
Key question: Does this information answer the task — or is it simply something else I understood from the text?

Use one short introductory sentence to identify the source and lead into the task focus.

No analysis hypothesis: In a comprehension task, the TATTT sentence identifies the source and topic only. An interpretation or analysis hypothesis belongs to an analysis task.
Example

Task: Outline how Trump presents America’s current situation and future development in the excerpt.

TATTT sentence: In his inaugural address delivered on January 20, 2025, Donald Trump presents his view of the United States and the direction he sees for the country’s future.

American English (AE)on January 20, 2025

Keep it concise: in the year 2025 is grammatically possible, but normally unnecessary — write in 2025. In formal continuous prose, prefer 20 January / January 20 rather than ordinal forms such as 20th.
  • Present the selected information in a clear and logical order.
  • Group related aspects instead of mechanically following the order of the source text.
  • Use your own words wherever possible.
  • Focus on the information itself rather than repeatedly writing what the author “states” or “explains”.
  • Stop once the task focus has been answered completely — there is no separate conclusion.
Tense: mainly simple present Use the simple present when referring to the text or presenting its ideas. Use another tense only when the content itself requires it.
Focus on content, not author phrases Author phrases such as The author states …, The author explains … or The author argues … are possible, but should be used sparingly. In a comprehension task, it is often better to present the relevant information directly, for example:
  • Many young Americans struggle with …
  • The main reason for … is …
  • According to the text, …
  • One important aspect is …
  • Another problem mentioned is …
Own words Paraphrase the relevant information instead of copying complete phrases or sentences from the source.
Formal and concise Use clear, formal language and avoid unnecessary repetition or filler phrases.
No analysis: Do not analyse stylistic devices, effects, functions or the line of argument.
No analysis hypothesis: Your introductory sentence should not interpret the text.
No line references: A comprehension answer does not normally require line numbers.
No unnecessary quotations: Paraphrase instead of proving points with quotations unless the task explicitly requires otherwise.
No personal opinion: Stay with the information required by the task.
No conclusion: End after you have answered the task focus completely.
No irrelevant information: Do not include details simply because they appear in the text.
