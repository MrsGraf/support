Skills training: Letter to the editor

A letter to the editor is a short, formal and persuasive response to an article, a public statement, a current event or an issue under public debate. It is addressed to the editor but written for the newspaper’s wider readership. Its purpose is to contribute a clear, well-supported perspective to a public discussion.

  • Add something useful: introduce new arguments, counter-arguments, another perspective, a personal experience, an example, a different solution or an outlook.
  • Do not merely summarise the article, statement or event. Refer to it only as far as necessary and focus on your own well-reasoned position.
  • Take a clear position. You do not have to present all sides equally. Focus on a few convincing arguments, but address relevant opposing views where appropriate.
  • Be concise and formal. Avoid contractions and slang; use precise and evaluative language instead.
Keep in mind: You are writing to the editor or newspaper, not directly to the author or speaker. You may use “I” and personal examples—this is your perspective.

Useful check: Does every paragraph contribute one clear idea? Remain factual, avoid personal attacks and provide enough context for the wider readership. Use brief quotations from an article or public statement only when they are directly relevant to your argument.

Starting the letter

  • I am writing to you in response to the article “...”
  • Having read your article ..., I would like to point out ...
  • I am writing in response to the recent debate about ...
  • I would like to comment on the statement that ...
  • President Trump’s claim that “...” raises the question of whether ...
  • Following the recent inaugural address, I would like to contribute a European perspective to the debate.

Agreeing and adding support

  • The article / statement resonates with my own experience.
  • The article / statement highlights a very important issue that ...
  • This vision is convincing because ...
  • I wholeheartedly agree with the idea that ...
  • As a firm believer in ..., I fully support ...
  • Although I understand why ..., I cannot accept your overall conclusion that ...

Disagreeing and challenging ideas

  • I see your point, but I still believe that ...
  • This vision may sound appealing, but ...
  • The argument overlooks the fact that ...
  • Supporters of this view may claim that ...; however, ...
  • From a European perspective, however, ...
  • The solution offered seems rather simplistic / one-sided / unrealistic.
  • I would like to question the idea / argument / notion that ...

Ending with a strong statement or outlook

  • At the end of the day, we all have to ...
  • So yes, we should ..., but we must not forget to also ...
  • The question should no longer be whether ..., but ...
  • All in all, there can be no doubt that ...
  • Ultimately, what matters is that ...
  • Consequently, I strongly support / oppose your view that ...

Final language check: Use formal vocabulary, avoid short forms such as don’t or can’t, and choose strong evaluative adjectives where appropriate.
