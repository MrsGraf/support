Skills training: Letter to the editor

A letter to the editor responds to an article that you agree or disagree with. Your aim is to make a clear, useful contribution to the discussion.

  • Add something useful: introduce new arguments, counter-arguments, another perspective, a personal experience, an example, a different solution or an outlook.
  • Do not simply praise or summarise the article. Refer to it briefly, then focus on your own opinion.
  • Select a few strong points. You do not need to present every argument from both sides. Ideally, address something the author has missed or left out.
  • Be concise and formal. Avoid contractions and slang; use precise and evaluative language instead.
Keep in mind: You are writing to the editor or newspaper, not directly to the author of the article. You may use “I” and personal examples—this is your perspective.

Useful check: Does every paragraph contribute your own idea? Quote from the article only when it is absolutely necessary.

Starting the letter

  • I am writing to you in response to the article “...”
  • Having read your article ..., I would like to point out ...
  • In your article ..., you claim that ...
  • Your article ... raises the question of whether / how / why ...

Agreeing and adding support

  • Your article resonates with my own experience.
  • The article highlights a very important issue that ...
  • You are absolutely right when you say ...
  • I wholeheartedly agree with the idea that ...
  • As a firm believer in ..., I fully support ...
  • Although I understand why ..., I cannot accept your overall conclusion that ...

Disagreeing and challenging ideas

  • I see your point, but I still believe that ...
  • I think you are mistaken if you believe ...
  • The solution you offer seems rather simplistic / one-sided / unrealistic.
  • What you need to keep in mind is ...
  • You overlook the fact that ...
  • I would like to question the idea / argument / notion that ...
  • However, does this evidence not suggest that ...?

Ending with a strong statement or outlook

  • At the end of the day, we all have to ...
  • So yes, we should ..., but we must not forget to also ...
  • The question should no longer be whether ..., but ...
  • All in all, there can be no doubt that ...
  • Ultimately, what matters is that ...
  • Consequently, I strongly support / oppose your view that ...

Final language check: Use formal vocabulary, avoid short forms such as don’t or can’t, and choose strong evaluative adjectives where appropriate.
